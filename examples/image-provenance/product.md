An image provenance tool for newsrooms: it traces where a photo or video frame first appeared, every copy and edit since, and how it spread. It matches near-duplicates across news archives, social platforms and web captures, and builds each image's family tree: the earliest known upload, the crops, filters, splices and recaptions made from it, and the route each copy took. Every link in the tree carries its evidence (visual similarity, metadata, timestamps, archive captures) and how strong that evidence is. People use it to find the first sighting of a picture, to see what changed between a copy and its parent, and to tell when a viral image is old, altered or taken out of context. Asking is a core feature: an agent answers questions in plain language ("when did this first appear?", "who cropped out the sign?") by querying the record.
What it is for: helping a journalist or fact-checker see the documented history of an image, including where the trail goes cold, where dates conflict, and which copies have no known source. The tool shows the record and how well each step is supported; it does not rule on whether an image is real or fake, and the person draws the conclusion.
Audience: journalists, fact-checkers, photo editors and archivists. It is not a deepfake detector, a photo-editing app, a stock-photo site or a consumer product.
Voice: technical and plain, the register of a verification desk's notes. No superlatives, no hype, no decorative metaphor. The tool holds evidence, not verdicts, so names must not overclaim: nothing that promises truth, proof or authenticity.
What makes a good name: it sounds like a sober technical term that ought to already exist, beside codex, lexicon, genome or archive. It is built from a classical root that an educated reader already half-knows through a common English word (vestige, stem, image, origin, trace), and the root names the product's structure or its work: a family tree of copies, a first sighting, a trail back to the source. A standard ending (-gram, -ome, -us, -eme, -ix, -dex) or prefix (sub-, inter-, retro-) shapes it, and the ending sounds natural on its root. It has a short, true answer to "why is it called that?", can be read aloud on first sight, and is spelled the way it sounds.
